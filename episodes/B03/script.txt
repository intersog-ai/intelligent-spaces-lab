Intersog presents. Intelligent Spaces. Build Track, episode three. Readings you can trust.

A thermometer in our kitchen got stuck. Same number, every minute, for twelve hours. Fresh timestamp, plausible value, no duplicates. A hub that trusted it made about six hundred heating decisions in one week on a number that was no longer true.

This is a research series. We take the home from season one and build it in the open, in a digital twin. The code, the data and the prompts are free to download. It is provided as is. Use it at your own risk, and for your own benefit.

Season one gave the home one rule for its senses. Fresh, sourced readings. Every reading has a device, a timestamp and an event ID. Stale or duplicate data must not drive a decision. This time we built that rule, and tested it.

We ran the twin for a simulated week, ten times, and broke the sensors on purpose. Duplicated messages. Messages out of order. A clock drifting two seconds an hour. A spike. A stuck thermometer. A dead battery. And a leak sensor that sends one alarm, then only says dry.

Three hubs read the same house at the same time. The first believes the latest message. The second checks device timestamps, like our first episode. The third uses a new reading quality layer. Same decisions, on the same data.

Lesson one. Timestamps fix the network, not the sensor. The timestamp hub removed all thirty weekly decisions made on duplicate or reordered messages. It still made the same six hundred decisions on the stuck thermometer. The new layer saw twenty minutes of identical values, switched to the second thermometer, and told the family.

Lesson two. An alarm is not cleared by a heartbeat that never said wet. When the leak sensor sent one alarm and then only dry reports, both simpler hubs reopened the valve. Water ran for about a hundred and twelve minutes. The layer kept the valve closed, flagged the sensor within about two and a half minutes, and asked a person to check.

Lesson three. Listen to the last gasp. A dying sensor reports battery zero before it goes silent. The layer caught it at once. Waiting for missed heartbeats took thirteen minutes. The simplest hub never noticed, and said all clear twice.

Lesson four. Do not trust a device clock, measure it. With a drift of two seconds an hour, the timestamp hub called a good thermometer stale after two to three days, and threw away about a hundred and ten hours of good readings a week. The layer estimated the offset at three hundred and thirty six seconds. It was right.

Trust has a cost. After the false dry reports, the valve stayed closed for over two hours after the floor was dry, until a person answered. A real five degree jump was held back for one report. Every time the layer refused to act, it said so. Over ten simulated weeks, thirty refusals, thirty messages.

Across the week, the new layer made two decisions on bad data, both from that one held report, and no false alarms. The simplest hub made six hundred and thirty seven, and almost five false alarms a week.

What is still open. Our sensors are very precise, which makes a stuck one easy to see. Real ones are coarser. And a rate limit would delay a real fire by one report, so fire stays with listed smoke alarms. The paper lists every limit.

The code, the data and the paper are in the Intelligent Spaces Lab repository, episode B03. If you build devices, platforms or spaces like these, Intersog's engineers do this work.
